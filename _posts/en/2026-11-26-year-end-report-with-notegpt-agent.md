At the end of the year, the hardest part of writing your report may be remembering what deserves to go into it.

Some results are easy to find: revenue, completed projects, and progress against targets. Other contributions take more work to explain—the process you improved, the teams you helped coordinate, or the recurring problem you finally resolved.

The evidence is often scattered across monthly updates, project documents, meeting notes, and spreadsheets. Before you can tell a clear story, you need to bring those pieces together.

A strong year-end report answers four questions:

- What did you accomplish?
- What evidence supports those accomplishments?
- What did you learn?
- What will you prioritize next?

[NoteGPT Agent](https://notegpt.io/ai-agent)’s five Skills—AI Sheets, Data Analysis & Charts, Deep Research, AI Documents, and AI Slides—can support each stage of that process. Here is a workflow you can adapt to your own records.

## 1. AI Sheets: Create an inventory of your work

Start with your records. Reviewing the year before drafting helps you find overlooked contributions and spot gaps in the evidence.

Use AI Sheets to organize your materials by project, month, or business area. For each item, capture the objective, your contribution, the outcome, and the records that support it.

**Try this prompt:**

> Using the uploaded work records, create an annual project summary table. Include each project’s name, objective, my contribution, completion status, key outcomes, and supporting source. Mark missing information as “Needs input.” Do not infer results that are not documented.

Review the table before moving on. Check project names, remove duplicates, and clarify where a team result needs to be distinguished from your individual contribution.

This inventory becomes the foundation for the rest of your report.

## 2. Data Analysis & Charts: Find the story in the numbers

With your records organized, you can ask more useful questions:

Which targets were met? Where did performance change? Which results need further explanation?

Use Data Analysis & Charts to examine those questions and prepare visuals that make the answers easier to understand.

**Try this prompt:**

> Analyze quarterly performance and progress against annual targets using the uploaded data. Create charts showing the most relevant trends and comparisons. Label time periods, units, and data sources. Flag missing data and separate findings supported by the records from possible explanations that need further investigation.

That final distinction matters. Performance may have improved after a process change, but timing alone does not establish what caused the improvement.

Before including a chart, check that the underlying comparisons are consistent. A percentage is only useful when the reader knows what it measures and how it was calculated.

Each chart should answer one clear question—and support a takeaway you can explain in a sentence.

## 3. Deep Research: Add context when it answers a question

External research can help explain the conditions in which your team worked. Industry developments, market shifts, and competitor updates may also inform next year’s priorities.

Use Deep Research when that context contributes to a specific part of your review.

**Try this prompt:**

> Research major developments in [industry] during [year], including relevant product updates from [three competitors]. Focus on changes that could inform our review of [business area] and next year’s planning. Include source links and publication dates. Clearly distinguish reported facts from interpretations.

Keep this section focused. A few relevant findings are more useful than a broad industry overview that distracts from your work.

External research can inform your interpretation of the year. Claims about your own results still need support from your internal records.

## 4. AI Documents: Turn the evidence into a coherent report

Once the facts and analysis are ready, use AI Documents to develop the written review.

A practical structure is:

- Annual objectives and overall results
- Key accomplishments and supporting evidence
- Challenges and lessons learned
- Priorities and actions for the coming year

Give contributions that are difficult to quantify enough space. A specific account of the problem, your action, and the documented outcome can explain value without an invented metric.

**Try this prompt:**

> Draft my annual review using the verified project table, analysis results, and relevant research. Organize it around objectives, accomplishments, challenges, lessons, and next-year priorities. Use metrics only where the records support them. For qualitative contributions, explain the problem, my action, and the documented outcome. Distinguish individual contributions from team results, and list any information I still need to provide.

When reviewing the draft, look for statements that sound impressive but say little. Replace “improved collaboration,” for example, with a concrete explanation of what changed and what evidence shows the difference.

## 5. AI Slides: Shape the report for your audience

The written review provides the detail. The presentation needs a clear sequence of takeaways that fits the meeting.

After verifying the report, use AI Slides to prepare a presentation around your audience’s needs and the time available.

**Try this prompt:**

> Turn the approved annual review into a 10-slide presentation for a 15-minute meeting with my department head. Open with the overall result, highlight three key accomplishments with supporting evidence, retain the essential charts, and cover the main challenges and next year’s priorities. Keep each slide focused on one main point.

Then refine the presentation. Move a key conclusion earlier, shorten the background, simplify a chart, or make the next steps more specific.

Check that every number matches the approved report and that each slide earns its place.
